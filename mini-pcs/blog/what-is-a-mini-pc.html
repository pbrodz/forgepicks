What exactly is a mini PC, and why would you want one?

Published October 23, 2026 · 7 min read

This week a deal site ran a headline we see constantly: an ASUS mini PC, "41% off" - $1,000, down from $1,699. Readers asked the obvious question: is that a good mini PC? The honest answer is that nobody can tell from the headline, because mini PC covers everything from an $80 hobby board to a $7,000 workstation the size of a sandwich. Until you know which tier a box sits in - and which features separate the tiers - a discount percentage tells you nothing. This page is the map.

Honesty box: prices below are typical street ranges as of October 2026 and move around constantly - the mini-PC posts on this site track the specific models. Verify before buying.

What a mini PC is (and is not)

A mini PC is a complete desktop computer - real CPU, RAM, SSD, and ports - shrunk into a box roughly the size of a paperback book. It runs a full operating system (Windows, Linux, or macOS), drives normal monitors, and does normal computer work. It is not a thin client that needs a bigger computer behind it, not a streaming stick, and not a gaming tower in disguise: the graphics are integrated, so AAA gaming at high settings is the one job it genuinely cannot do.

Why would you want one? For the job this site cares about - a home server - the magic numbers are size and idle power. A mini PC idles at roughly 6-12 watts where an old desktop tower idles at 40-80, and it runs silently on a shelf instead of humming under a desk. Left on 24/7, that difference is real money every year (we did the math in our idle power guide).

The five tiers

Every mini PC on the market falls into one of five bands. The tiers matter more than any single spec, because a great price in the wrong tier is still the wrong box.

Notice what the tiers are really pricing: not raw speed, but headroom - more cores, more RAM capacity, more ports, more storage slots. Most home servers use a fraction of even a budget box. Buy the tier that fits the job you will actually run a year from now, not the job you daydream about.

The five features that actually separate the tiers

1. RAM: socketed or soldered

The single most important line on the spec sheet. Socketed (SODIMM) RAM can be upgraded in two years for pocket money. Soldered RAM - common on laptop chips dropped into mini PCs - is whatever you bought, forever. That $1,000 ASUS in the headline? A capable machine, but its 32GB is soldered to a laptop processor: at power-tier money, we want sockets. Rule of thumb: the more you pay, the more this matters.

2. Storage slots

One M.2 slot is a ceiling; two is a plan. Servers accumulate drives - a fast SSD for the system, a bigger one for media. Check how many M.2 slots a box has and whether there is a 2.5-inch bay before the price seduces you.

3. Ports that matter for a homelab

2.5GbE networking is the 2026 baseline - a "server" with only gigabit ethernet is last generation. Dual LAN opens up router and firewall builds. USB4 / Thunderbolt means fast external storage and docks. OCuLink (on power-tier boxes like the K11) means an external GPU later, if local AI or transcoding outgrows the integrated chip.

4. Idle power and noise

A 24/7 box lives where you live. Idle draw under ~12W and a fan you cannot hear from the couch are worth more than two extra benchmark points. This is where the budget tier embarrasses old desktops, and where the Mac mini is quietly the best-engineered box on the list.

5. Barebone or complete

Barebone boxes (no RAM, SSD, or OS) look dramatically cheaper - until you price the parts. Our standing rule, learned on the UM890 Pro versus K11 comparison: barebone only wins if you already own the RAM, SSD, and Windows license. Otherwise the "expensive" complete box is frequently the cheaper machine. (Rising memory prices make this worse - see our RAM price tracker.)

Why you might NOT want a mini PC

Honesty cuts both ways. There are real jobs where the mini PC is simply the wrong tool, and no tier fixes it:

  • You game seriously. Integrated graphics is integrated graphics. A small-form-factor desktop with a real GPU card will outplay any mini PC at the same money, and it is not close.
  • You hoard media. Mini PCs have no 3.5-inch drive bays. If your library wants four big hard drives with room for four more, that machine is called a NAS or a tower - a mini PC plus a pile of USB enclosures is a compromise, not a plan.
  • You upgrade one part at a time, for a decade. The CPU is soldered to the board, the board is proprietary, the power supply is a laptop brick. A standard ATX build is a ship you can rebuild plank by plank; a mini PC is a boat you replace.
  • Your workloads run flat-out for hours. Long renders, big compiles, sustained all-core work: laptop-class cooling throttles where a tower's big fans hold boost clocks all day.
  • You are shopping at the top of the range. Past roughly $900, a small desktop usually gives you more computer for the money. Up there, the mini premium buys size and silence - which may be exactly what you want, but know that is what you are paying for.

If two or more of those sound like you, buy the other machine with our blessing. The mini PC is a specialist: unbeatable at quiet, efficient, always-on work, and genuinely the wrong answer outside it.

Three traps to walk past

  • The MSRP mirage. "41% off" a launch price nobody has paid in a year is marketing, not a deal. Compare against the street price of the tier, not the sticker in the headline.
  • Soldered everything at power prices. Laptop chips in desktop boxes bring laptop limits: fixed RAM, fixed storage, premium cost. Fine at budget prices, a bad trade at $1,000.
  • Halo specs. 192GB of RAM, workstation GPUs, five-figure TOPS counts - specs written for press releases. NPU "AI TOPS" in particular: almost no home-server software can use an NPU today. Buy cores, sockets, and slots instead.

The bottom line

A mini PC is the right computer for a home server because it sips power, stays silent, and disappears onto a shelf. Pick by tier, check the five features - RAM sockets above all - and let the discount headlines do their worst. For most households the answer lands in the budget or mainstream tier, well under $500, which is exactly where our shortlist spends most of its time.
