Mini PC + NAS: how they work together (and when one box is enough)

Published October 23, 2026 · 7 min read

Read any mini PC listing closely and the word NAS shows up - "great as a NAS," "pair with your NAS," "NAS-ready." It is rarely explained. Here is the actual relationship: a NAS and a mini PC are not competitors. One holds your files; the other does your computing. Homelabs mix them in three patterns, and knowing which pattern you are in answers most buying questions before they start. (New to the mini PC half? Start with what a mini PC is.)

Honesty box: this is a patterns guide, not a product roundup - we do not rank NAS boxes here. Prices mentioned are typical 2026 street ranges for orientation, and they move.

First, what a NAS actually is

NAS stands for network-attached storage: a small computer whose entire job is to hold drives and serve their contents over your network, 24/7. Other machines open its folders like local ones (via SMB or NFS shares). A real NAS adds RAID - mirroring or parity across drives, so one drive dying costs you an afternoon, not your photo library. You can buy one as an appliance (Synology and QNAP are the big names) or build one on NAS software like TrueNAS or Unraid. Either way: storage is the mission, and the processors inside buyable NAS appliances are deliberately weak - they sip power and move files, and that is all they are asked to do.

Pattern 1: the NAS does it all (and for most people, it works)

Start with the setup nobody should talk you out of: a NAS running its own apps. Modern appliances run Plex or Jellyfin natively, and the Intel-based models carry the same Quick Sync hardware transcoding as a mini PC - for the classic one-or-two-streams household, Plex on a NAS is not a compromise, it is the mainstream setup, and it works well. NAS operating systems also run Docker containers, so the supporting cast lives there too: monitoring, downloaders, photo services, dashboards.

A concrete homelab built exactly this way: a NAS running Plex plus an Uptime Kuma container, and a Raspberry Pi on the side running Pi-hole. The Kuma container watches the Pi-hole; the NAS serves the movies; the Pi filters the ads. No mini PC anywhere - and nothing about it needs fixing. If your services are the classic set and your library fits the bays, this pattern is not a stepping stone. It is a destination.

Its honest limits are about scale, not viability: software-transcoding several 4K streams at once, a rack of virtual machines, or CPU-hungry applications will outgrow an appliance processor. That is an upgrade path, not a flaw - and it leads to pattern 2.

Pattern 2: the mini PC is the brain, the NAS is the vault

When the apps outgrow the NAS, the fix is not replacing it - it is promoting it. The NAS keeps the media, backups, and files. A mini PC mounts those folders over the network and takes over everything that needs a brain: heavier transcoding loads, bigger Docker stacks, virtual machines.

The connection between them matters more than people expect. If the brain reads its media from the vault, the network link is the drive cable - which is why we treat 2.5GbE as the homelab baseline, and why dual-LAN mini PCs exist: one port for the network, one that can talk straight to storage.

Pattern 3: the mini PC IS the NAS

Install TrueNAS, Unraid, or OpenMediaVault on a mini PC with enough drive slots and it becomes the NAS - one box doing both jobs. Done with SSDs, it is silent, fast, and idles at mini-PC watts, which no drive-bay appliance can match.

The ceiling is capacity and cost per terabyte. Mini PCs take M.2 and 2.5-inch drives, not the big cheap 3.5-inch hard drives a NAS bay swallows. An all-SSD mini-PC NAS is lovely up to a few terabytes; a growing 20TB media library wants spinning disks at a third of the price per terabyte. Storage math, not performance, is what pushes people to pattern 2.

Pattern 4: the NAS as the safety net

Even when the mini PC does everything else, a NAS earns its shelf space as the backup target: Proxmox and Docker hosts back up their virtual machines and containers to the NAS nightly. Brain dies? Restore onto any other box. It is the least glamorous pattern and the one people thank themselves for after the first dead drive.

The shortcut to avoid: drives on USB

The tempting hack - hang a stack of USB drives off a mini PC and call it a NAS - is the one setup the storage community warns about near-unanimously. USB enclosures hide drive health data (SMART), bridges drop connections under load, and RAID or ZFS pools built on them corrupt in ways that are hard to diagnose. A single USB drive for backups is fine. A USB drive pool as your only copy of anything is how data dies quietly.

The bottom line

A NAS and a mini PC are a vault and a brain - and plenty of homelabs, including well-run ones we know, let the vault do the thinking too. If a NAS already runs your Plex and your containers happily, there is nothing to fix: standalone NAS is a real architecture, not a starter kit. The mini PC enters when the services outgrow the appliance, or when you want the storage and the compute on separate upgrade paths. Start where you are; let the workload, not the marketing, call for the second box.
